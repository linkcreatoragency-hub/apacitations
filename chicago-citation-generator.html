What is Chicago style?

Chicago style, defined by The Chicago Manual of Style (17th edition), is the standard citation format in history, philosophy, and much of academic publishing. It actually offers two systems:

  • Notes–Bibliography: numbered footnotes or endnotes in the text, plus a bibliography at the end. Common in history and the humanities.
  • Author–Date: parenthetical citations like (Kahneman 2011, 24), plus a reference list. Common in the sciences and social sciences.

This generator produces bibliography entries — the full-detail format used in both systems — along with an author–date in-text citation.

Chicago citation examples

Website
Chowdhury, Rina. "The Health Benefits of Walking Every Day." Healthline. March 12, 2024. https://www.healthline.com/health/benefits-of-walking.
Book
Kahneman, Daniel. Thinking, Fast and Slow. Farrar, Straus and Giroux, 2011.
Journal article
Rasch, Björn, and Jan Born. "About Sleep's Role in Memory." Physiological Reviews 93, no. 2 (2013): 681–766. https://doi.org/10.1152/physrev.00032.2012.

Key Chicago rules to remember

Invert only the first author's name ("Last, First, and First Last"). Titles of books and journals are italicized; article and chapter titles go in "quotation marks." In the bibliography, entries are alphabetized by the first author's last name and formatted with a hanging indent. Dates are written in full — March 12, 2024 — unlike APA's (2024, March 12).

Frequently asked questions

Does this tool do Chicago footnotes?
The generator produces bibliography entries plus author–date in-text citations. A footnote uses the same information with slightly different punctuation: first name first, commas instead of periods, and the page cited — e.g., Daniel Kahneman, Thinking, Fast and Slow (Farrar, Straus and Giroux, 2011), 24.
What's the difference between Chicago and Turabian?
Turabian is a simplified version of Chicago style designed for students. The citation formats are essentially identical, so citations from this generator work for Turabian papers too.
When should I use Chicago style?
Chicago is most common in history, philosophy, religion, and the arts, and it is the house style of many book publishers. Your syllabus or instructor will normally specify it.
