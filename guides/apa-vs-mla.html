APA vs MLA: Key Differences Explained

By the APACitations Editorial Team · Updated August 2026

APA and MLA are the two most common citation styles in education — and mixing them up is one of the most common formatting errors in student papers. Here's a clear side-by-side comparison, plus a simple rule for knowing which one you need.

In-text citations: author–date vs author–page

The deepest difference is what goes in parentheses. APA emphasizes when research was published; MLA emphasizes where in the text:

APA: (Kahneman, 2011, p. 24) · MLA: (Kahneman 24)

APA uses a comma and "p."; MLA uses neither. APA shortens 3+ authors to "et al." always; MLA does the same from the first citation too, but with different punctuation in the Works Cited entry.

References vs Works Cited

The same book, both ways:

APA: Kahneman, D. (2011). Thinking, fast and slow. Farrar, Straus and Giroux.
MLA: Kahneman, Daniel. Thinking, Fast and Slow. Farrar, Straus and Giroux, 2011.

Spot the differences: APA inverts to initials (Kahneman, D.), puts the year right after the author, and uses sentence case. MLA spells out the full first name, puts the year last, and capitalizes titles in Title Case.

The mistakes people make when switching

Students who learned one style first predictably slip when writing in the other. Coming from MLA to APA, the classic errors are Title Case in reference titles, spelled-out first names, and missing years in in-text citations. Coming from APA to MLA: adding a comma before the page number — (Morrison, 27) is wrong in MLA — writing "p." before pages, and using sentence case in Works Cited titles. When you switch styles mid-degree, re-generate your references rather than hand-editing them; punctuation differences are too easy to miss.

Frequently asked questions

Can I mix APA and MLA in one paper?
No — a paper follows exactly one style throughout, covering in-text citations, the source list, and page formatting. Mixing styles is treated as a formatting error.
Is APA or MLA easier to learn?
MLA has fewer moving parts (no publication years in-text, one core-element template), while APA has more specific rules per source type. In practice, whichever your field uses daily becomes easiest.
Which style do high schools use?
MLA is most common in high school English classes in North America, which is why many students meet it first. University science and social science courses then require APA.
